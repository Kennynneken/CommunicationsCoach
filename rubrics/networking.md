# Rubric — Networking Event

100 = historic-best room presence: remembered by everyone, effortless authority.

## Weighting
- Memorability & novelty (script-breaking in small doses; stands out from the
  autopilot crowd) — 25%
- Leadership signals (sharp questions, energy, vocal presence in groups) — 25%
- Emotional pivots (turns credential-talk into story-talk) — 20%
- Composure (breath before approach, certainty in voice, low filler) — 15%
- Body language (upright approach posture, smile ON the words, gestures in the
  Power Sphere) — 15%

## The approach beat (cold open — score with THIS section, not the general one)

A cold approach is its own beat with its own goal function. Per Smithyman:

- **The goal is the Mediocre First Impression: stranger → non-stranger.** A
  cold open is NOT scored on brilliance of material. It is scored on:
  1. **Warmth out, on time** — warmth is reflected; he must emit first. Smile
     ON the words (face is the remote control of tone), volume at 5-of-10.
  2. **Goal-appropriateness** — a plain, basic introduction with a name is
     world-class approach technique. Over-engineered openers signal
     performance anxiety, and high performance demands RAISE anxiety.
  3. **A door opened** — did he end with something the other person can walk
     through (a question, a thread, a handle), and did he leave the silence
     for them to walk through it? Answering his own opener = burying the door.
  4. **Safety behaviors named and penalized**: padding clauses, speed,
     approval-checking, apologizing for existing ("sorry to interrupt"),
     over-explaining the approach itself.
- **Do not demand unexpectedness in the first ten seconds.** Script-breaking
  is earned *inside* a conversation; at the door, warm + plain + certain beats
  clever. (RDJ's script transcendence requires knowing the script first —
  the approach IS the script part.)
- Group entries: feed the live thread, don't restart it; enter at the widest
  gap; eye contact with a listener, not the speaker.

## Notes (in-conversation)
- The doctor test — Duhigg's own verbatim example: "Where did you go to
  medical school?" = fail; **"What made you decide to become a doctor?"** =
  pass. Deep questions ask about values, decisions, experiences, feelings
  about life — not facts of life. Search the transcript for which one he ran.
- **Follow-up questions are the highest-value move in the room** (Huang et
  al.: follow-ups drive likability more than any other question type, because
  they prove listening). Count them: a follow-up is built from THEIR exact
  words, not from his agenda.
- **Reciprocity braid:** questions alone cap the score (liked, not known —
  Smithyman). His answers must disclose: motive ("because"), contrast
  ("but"), current motion ("lately"), a concrete picture ("for example").
  When he's asked "what do you do," a scene beats a category, and one layer
  below the surface — personal, not private.
- **Tempo: score contrast, not a WPM zone.** Fast-with-melody-and-pauses is a
  legitimate elite profile (RDJ). Score DOWN: one unvarying rate, zero pauses
  after his own questions, self-burial. Score UP: a deliberate slow-down on
  the line that matters, silence held after asking.
- In group audio, measure whether his voice carries: intensity relative to
  others, and whether his questions redirect the group thread.
- Filler per minute > 5 in a first-impression context is a priority fix; the
  fix is a pause, not suppression (Giang).

## Common score bands
- 80s: approached warm and plain, braided questions with disclosure, one
  script-break that landed, silence held where it mattered
- 60s: competent entry, real questions, but interrogation-mode or
  monologue-mode; tempo at one speed
- 40s: autopilot questions, safety behaviors visible, warmth late or absent,
  buried his own openers
