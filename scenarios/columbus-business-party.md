# Scenario Deck — Columbus Business Party / Networking Event

Fourteen situations you will actually hit at a Columbus business mixer, a
company holiday party, a Chamber event, a Startup Week after-party, or a
client dinner that turns into a room. Each card is a drill, not a script.

**How to use a card**
1. Read the *Moment* and the *Autopilot* version. The autopilot version is what
   you'll do by default under mild social pressure. Naming it is half the fix.
2. Practice the *Move* out loud — record 60–90 seconds into your phone
   (you playing both sides is fine; the metrics that matter here are yours).
3. Drop the take in `sessions/`, run it with the tagged context, get scored.
4. After the real event, record a 2-minute voice debrief the same night and run
   that too — memory of what you actually said decays by morning.

**Rubric mapping.** Most of these score under `networking`
(memorability, leadership signals, emotional pivots, composure, body language).
Two-person, seated, no agenda → `small-talk`. Seated meal → `dinner-party`.
Standing up to say words → `speech`. Each card is tagged.

## ACT I — The First Ninety Seconds

### S1 · The Doorway Reset
**Where:** Land-Grant taproom in Franklinton, 6:10pm, Chamber after-hours. The
room is already at full volume and you walked in alone.
**Moment:** You're in the doorway holding your coat, scanning for a familiar
face, and the scan is visible from across the room.
**Autopilot (scores 35–45):** Beeline to the bar, phone out, drink as a prop,
first real conversation at minute 22 with whoever ends up next to you.
**The move:**
- Stop just inside the door. One full breath in, out. Two-second pause with
  your eyes up, not on your phone. You are allowed to look at a room.
- Shoulders back, coat over the arm, chin level. Decide on a target before you
  move — an open pair or a person alone at the rail.
- Talk to someone within 90 seconds of walking in. The first conversation is a
  warm-up rep; it does not have to be the important one.
**In the pocket:** *"I'm going to be honest, I don't know a soul here yet — I'm
Kenny."* Admitting it is disarming; scanning silently is not.
**Coach's eye:** composure and approach posture. In the recording I'm reading
your first 20 seconds of speech for filler density and whether your opening
sentence has a downward, certain ending or a rising, asking-permission one.
**Context tag:** `networking`

---

### S2 · Breaking Into the Closed Triangle
**Where:** Wolf's Ridge, three people in a tight circle, mid-laugh.
**Moment:** They're closed off, shoulder to shoulder. You want in.
**Autopilot (scores 30–40):** Hover at the edge for 40 seconds waiting to be
noticed, then interject into the middle of someone's sentence and reset the
whole thread to introductions.
**The move:**
- Approach the *widest gap* in the circle, stand at the edge, angle your body
  in — don't wedge. Make eye contact with whoever is *listening*, not whoever
  is talking. Listeners have the bandwidth to let you in.
- Laugh or react honestly at the right beat first. You earn the floor by being
  in the conversation before you speak in it.
- Your first words should feed their thread, not restart it: *"Wait, back up —
  what happened after that?"* You are now a participant, not an interruption.
**Never:** "Sorry to interrupt." You just did, and now you've announced it.
**Coach's eye:** timing of your first utterance relative to a sentence boundary
in the group audio, and whether your entry question redirected or extended.
**Context tag:** `networking`

---

### S3 · "So, What Do You Do?"
**Where:** Everywhere, within four minutes, every single time.
**Moment:** The most-asked question in Columbus business rooms and the biggest
missed opportunity in the whole deck.
**Autopilot (scores 40–50):** Job title, company, maybe a department. The other
person nods, says "oh nice," and now you're both hunting for the next topic.
**The move:** Answer with a *scene*, not a *category*. Two beats, then hand it
back. Titles are filing systems; scenes are memorable.
- Weak: "I'm in sales at [company]."
- Strong: *"I spend most of my week talking people out of things they were
  about to buy. Turns out it's the fastest way to get them to buy the right
  one."* — pause — *"What about you, what's actually eating your week?"*
- "What's eating your week" beats "what do you do" because it gets a *this
  week* answer, and this-week answers have emotion attached.
**Coach's eye:** length of your self-description (over ~20 seconds and it's a
monologue), and whether you handed the turn back with a question or let it die.
**Context tag:** `networking`

## ACT II — Getting Off the Surface

### S4 · The Doctor Test, Columbus Edition
**Where:** A hospital-system table — Nationwide Children's, OhioHealth, Wexner.
In this town you *will* meet healthcare people; it's the biggest employment
block in the room.
**Moment:** "I'm a pediatric cardiologist at Children's."
**Autopilot (scores 40–45):** "Oh wow, where'd you go to med school?" →
"How long have you been there?" → "Do you like it?" Three questions, zero
emotion, and you are now indistinguishable from every other person they met.
**The move:** Skip the résumé entirely. Go at motivation, then cost, then
identity — three layers.
- L1: *"What made you pick hearts? Of all of it — why hearts?"*
- L2 (on their answer): *"Was there a specific kid, or was it more gradual
  than that?"*
- L3: *"What does a day like that do to you when you go home?"*
**The rule:** every answer contains the next question. If you're reaching for a
new topic, you skipped a layer.
**Coach's eye:** I count layers per thread in the transcript and cite the exact
moment you bailed to a new topic. Target: at least one three-layer thread per
conversation.
**Context tag:** `small-talk`

---

### S5 · The Buckeye Reflex
**Where:** Any room, any month, but lethal in the fall.
**Moment:** Someone opens with "O-H—" or launches into the Michigan game.
**Autopilot (scores 35–45):** You trade takes on the quarterback situation.
Pleasant, frictionless, completely forgettable — you just had the same
conversation forty other people had tonight.
**The move:** Accept the ritual, then convert sports into story in one question.
Sports talk is a *handshake*, not a conversation. Shake and move.
- *"I-O."* — then, without pausing long: *"What's the best game you've actually
  been inside the Shoe for? Not the best team — the best night."*
- That reframe ("not the best team, the best night") is the whole move. It
  forces a memory instead of an opinion, and memories come with feeling.
- If they didn't grow up here: *"Who did you root for before you got adopted
  by this place?"* — now you're in origin-story territory.
**Coach's eye:** novelty score. Generic sports takes are the single most common
autopilot pattern in Midwest business rooms, and I flag every one.
**Context tag:** `networking`

---

### S6 · "Are You From Here?"
**Where:** Second question in half of all Columbus conversations, because this
city is half transplants and half people who've never left.
**Moment:** They ask you, or you ask them, and it dead-ends into geography.
**Autopilot (scores 40–50):** "Yeah, grew up here." / "No, moved from Chicago
in 2019." Two facts exchanged. Thread over.
**The move:** Fork it — both branches lead somewhere emotional.
- **Transplant:** *"What made you actually pull the trigger on moving? Nobody
  moves to a new city for a neutral reason."* Then: *"What surprised you? Be
  honest — good or bad."*
- **Native:** *"So you've watched the whole thing change. What's the one thing
  about old Columbus you're genuinely sad about?"* Nostalgia and grievance are
  both emotional fuel, and people love being asked to be the expert on their
  own hometown.
**In the pocket:** *"Where do you take people when they visit and you want to
show off?"* — a novel question that never gets a boring answer.
**Coach's eye:** question classification — informational vs. emotional vs.
identity. This card is where most people go 3-for-3 informational.
**Context tag:** `small-talk`

### S7 · The Big Fish, Ninety Seconds
**Where:** A Drive Capital partner, a Nationwide VP, the founder everybody in
the room is orbiting. You get them alone by the coat rack.
**Moment:** Ninety seconds of real attention and a strong instinct to pitch.
**Autopilot (scores 30–40):** Compressed elevator pitch delivered at 190 WPM
with no pauses, because you can feel the window closing. They've heard eleven
of these tonight and remember none of them.
**The move:** Do not pitch. Be the one person who didn't.
- Breath. One beat of silence *before* you speak — that pause alone separates
  you from everyone who lunged.
- One real question, asked slowly: *"What's the thing you keep having to
  explain to people that you wish they already understood?"* Executives are
  starved for a question that isn't an ask.
- Listen fully. Then leave *first*: *"I'm going to let you get back to the room
  — I'd like to keep talking about this. Can I follow up?"* Leaving first is
  the highest-status move at the table.
**Coach's eye:** WPM spike and filler rate under pressure. This is where your
composure metric will crack if it's going to. Target: under 5 fillers/min and
WPM within 15 of your relaxed baseline.
**Context tag:** `networking`

---

### S8 · The Macro Table (Intel, Honda, "the economy")
**Where:** A high-top where four people are trading headlines — the fab out in
Licking County, who's hiring, who's on a hiring freeze, interest rates.
**Moment:** The conversation is entirely abstract. Nobody has said "I" in five
minutes. Everyone is safe, and everyone is bored.
**Autopilot (scores 45–55):** Add your own headline take. It's competent and it
changes nothing — you become the fifth voice in an op-ed.
**The move:** Be the first person to make it personal. One question does it.
- *"Alright, but for you specifically — has any of that actually changed what
  you're doing day to day? Or is it all still just news?"*
- Or aim at hope: *"If it all goes the way people keep saying it will, what
  does that mean for you in three years?"* People crave the out-of-the-ordinary
  question, and in a macro conversation the out-of-the-ordinary move is
  simply *specificity*.
- Then shut up and let the table reorganize around the new thread. That
  reorganization is the leadership signal.
**Coach's eye:** in group audio I'm checking whether your question *redirected
the thread* — did the next two speakers follow your frame? That's 25% of the
networking rubric.
**Context tag:** `networking`

## ACT III — Owning the Group

### S9 · Reviving the Dying Circle
**Where:** Five people, 8:40pm, energy sagging. One person has checked their
phone twice. Someone says "yeah…" and nobody follows.
**Moment:** The circle is about to dissolve and everyone knows it.
**Autopilot (scores 35–45):** You let it dissolve, or you fill the gap with a
low-energy logistics comment ("parking was rough tonight") that confirms
everyone's exit.
**The move:** Raise your energy *above* the room's, not to match it. Groups
adopt the highest-conviction voice available.
- Lift your chin, half-step in, and ask something with stakes:
  *"Okay, actual question for the group — what's the best thing that's happened
  to any of you this year? Work or not."*
- Or the adventure angle: *"Someone here has a wild story they're not telling.
  Who's been somewhere in the last year that surprised them?"*
- Then *name a person to answer first*: "Sarah, you're going." Groups stall
  because nobody wants to go first. Choosing for them is leadership.
**Coach's eye:** relative vocal intensity vs. other speakers, plus pitch
variance. Reviving a room with a monotone voice is not possible; melody is the
mechanism.
**Context tag:** `networking`

### S10 · You Get Steamrolled
**Where:** Group of six, and the loudest guy talks over the back half of your
sentence for the second time.
**Moment:** You feel the heat rise and you have three bad options: retreat,
match his volume, or make it a joke at your own expense.
**Autopilot (scores 30–40):** Trail off, cede the floor, laugh along. Your
sentence-final intensity collapses and every person in the circle registers it
without knowing why.
**The move:** Stillness plus reclamation. Do not get louder — get *slower*.
- Stop talking mid-sentence. Hold. Let his sentence land into your silence.
  Stay physically still and keep your eyes on him, pleasant face.
- When he finishes: *"I want to come back to what you just said —"* pause —
  *"but let me finish the thought first, it's short."* Warm tone, zero apology,
  full sentence-final drop.
- Deliver your point at 80% of your normal speed. Slow reads as certain.
**Coach's eye:** intensity trail-off percentage — the metric that catches you
fading out of your own sentences. Also incongruence: warm words with collapsed
posture is the tell that you actually retreated.
**Context tag:** `networking`

---

### S11 · The Two-Second Dead Air
**Where:** Just you and one other person at a high-top, and the thread just
died. Both of you are looking at your drinks.
**Moment:** Silence at second two, and it is starting to have a temperature.
**Autopilot (scores 35–45):** Panic-fill with an autopilot question ("so how
long have you been with the company?") or the terminal move — "well, I'm going
to grab another drink."
**The move:** Silence in a *speech* is power; silence in *small talk* past two
seconds is a liability — but the fix is warmth, not speed.
- Smile first, then speak. A genuine smile before the sentence changes how the
  sentence sounds; you can hear it in the pitch data.
- Go sideways and personal, never back to logistics: *"Totally unrelated —
  what's something interesting that's happened to you lately?"* or
  *"What are you actually excited about right now? Doesn't have to be work."*
- The "totally unrelated" preface gives you permission to hard-cut topics
  without it feeling like a non sequitur. Keep it in the pocket.
**Coach's eye:** count of mid-conversation silences over 2.0s, and what
immediately follows each one — a filler, a question, or a retreat.
**Context tag:** `small-talk`

## ACT IV — Landing It

### S12 · The Graceful Exit (with a Handoff)
**Where:** Any conversation that's run its course at minute twelve.
**Moment:** You want out without making them feel discarded.
**Autopilot (scores 40–50):** "Well, I'll let you go." — which tells them
*they* were the one being tolerated. Or the bathroom fade, which they notice.
**The move:** Close warm, close specific, and if you can, hand them to someone.
- Name the value: *"This was the best conversation I've had tonight — the thing
  about your dad's shop is going to stick with me."* Specific beats effusive.
- Handoff: *"Come here, you have to meet Dana — she spent two years doing
  exactly what you're about to do."* You just did both of them a favor and you
  are now the person who connects people, which is the most durable reputation
  in a business community this size.
- Next step, concrete: *"I'll send you that thing Thursday."* Then actually
  send it Thursday.
**Coach's eye:** sentence-final cadence on the closing lines. Exits delivered
with a rising, uncertain ending undo an otherwise strong conversation.
**Context tag:** `networking`

### S13 · "Say a Few Words" (the Ambush Toast)
**Where:** Someone taps a glass, the room turns, and it's you — a client dinner
at a Short North place, or the office party where you're expected to speak.
**Moment:** Thirty to sixty seconds, no notes, everyone looking.
**Autopilot (scores 40–50):** Start talking on the walk up, at high speed,
listing thank-yous, and end with "…so, yeah. Cheers." — the whole thing
delivered at 180+ WPM with no pause anywhere.
**The move:** The structure is fixed, so pre-load it — one specific moment, one
meaning, one raise of the glass.
- **Before a word:** get to your spot, plant both feet, breathe, and hold
  1.5 seconds of silence while looking at the room. The silence buys the
  attention; talking over the noise never does.
- **Beat 1 (a moment):** *"Three weeks ago, at 11pm, Marcus was still on the
  phone with a customer who wasn't even his account."*
- **Beat 2 (the meaning):** pause 1.5s. *"That's the whole company, right
  there."*
- **Beat 3 (the raise):** *"To the people who stay on the phone."*
- Full stop at the end of every sentence. Under a minute. Sit down.
**Coach's eye:** this one scores under the speech rubric — pause count ≥1.2s at
beat boundaries, WPM in the 110–150 power zone, zero trailing "…so, yeah."
**Context tag:** `speech`

### S14 · The Same-Night Debrief (your actual homework)
**Where:** Your car in the parking garage, or the drive home down 315.
**Moment:** The event is over and you think you'll remember it. You won't.
**The move:** Voice memo, two to three minutes, before you get home. Answer
these five out loud — this is the recording I want:
1. Who did I meet, and what's the one *emotional* thing I know about each one?
   (If it's a job title, that conversation stayed on the surface.)
2. Where did I go three layers deep — and where did I bail at layer one?
3. What question did I ask that I've asked a hundred times before?
4. When did I feel my energy drop, and what happened right before it?
5. What's the one follow-up I'm actually sending, to whom, by when?
**Why it's the highest-value recording in this deck:** it's unscripted speech
under mild fatigue, which is the most honest sample of your baseline vocal
habits the pipeline ever gets — and it doubles as your CRM.
**Context tag:** `small-talk` (score it as conversational baseline)

## The Columbus Hazard List

Five patterns specific to this market, each a fast way to become forgettable:

1. **The Ohio State handshake loop.** School, year, dorm, game takes. It builds
   familiarity and zero intimacy. Ritual first, then convert (S5).
2. **Company-name-as-personality.** In a town of a handful of huge employers —
   Nationwide, Cardinal, Huntington, Honda, the hospital systems, OSU — half
   the room defines itself by a logo. Ask about the *work*, never the badge.
3. **Second-city defensiveness.** "Columbus is actually really great now" is a
   script this city runs on visitors. Don't run it, and when it's run at you,
   go personal: *"What made you stay, though?"*
4. **Everyone knows everyone.** Small market, two degrees max. Your reputation
   travels faster than your résumé, so the connector move in S12 compounds
   here more than it would in Chicago or New York.
5. **Politeness as a ceiling.** Midwest business rooms are extremely pleasant
   and extremely surface. Pleasant is the *floor*, not the goal — nobody
   remembers pleasant. The differentiator is the one person who asked a real
   question.

## Self-Score Card (fill this out in the debrief)

Five counts, before I score the recording. Say the numbers out loud in the
voice memo:

| # | Count | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Threads where you reached layer 3 | ≥ 1 per conversation |
| 2 | Novel questions asked (not in your usual rotation) | ≥ 3 per event |
| 3 | Times you paused and breathed *before* taking a turn | ≥ 5 |
| 4 | Conversations you exited with a concrete next step | ≥ 2 |
